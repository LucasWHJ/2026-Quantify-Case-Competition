# Product and pilot design

## Proposed customer promise

Develop a clearly defined AI endorsement for selected existing SME professional-service and technology customers. It would affirm coverage for disclosed, verified uses when covered errors cause third-party financial loss, subject to the responding underlying policy.

Before charging an additional premium, show the customer what changes from the existing policy: an affirmative coverage grant, a specified exclusion buyback, an explicit AI sublimit or agreed defence arrangements. Existing E&O might already respond to some AI-assisted professional errors. An endorsement needs identifiable incremental value.

## Initial eligibility

Accept a risk for assessment only when all of these can be established:

1. The customer's role, AI purpose and affected population are declared.
2. Human review is substantive and evidenced before consequential external action.
3. The alleged harm maps to the intended E&O or Technology Products policy.
4. Bodily injury, physical-system operations, autonomous consequential actions, IP and other excluded pathways remain outside the initial grant unless specifically approved.
5. Model/vendor dependency and affected-client scale fit an approved concentration budget.

Failure mechanism and oversight are separate dimensions. Four of the nine autonomous-failure incidents record oversight as Yes. A supervision flag does not establish low autonomy or prevent large loss.

Technology Products Liability in the case is not assumed to be identical to Tech E&O. Coverage for nonphysical software losses must be confirmed in the actual wording.

| Scenario | Initial treatment | Underwriting evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Human-reviewed professional report causes covered financial harm | Candidate | Service definition, review logs, error exposure, existing policy response |
| Embedded AI software causes third-party economic loss | Specialist review | Developer/deployer role, user count, product wording, change controls |
| Hiring or lending discrimination | Refer, rather than automatic inclusion | Discrimination/employment coverage, group claims, testing and legal review |
| Medical, vehicle, robotic or autonomous deployment/transfer use | Outside ordinary initial pilot | Physical harm, consequential permissions, common-event capacity |
| Privacy breach, deepfake fraud or cyber exploitation | Coordinate with specialist cover | Liability versus first-party loss, Cyber exclusions, policy overlap |

## Contract decisions before binding

- Claims-made versus occurrence trigger, retroactive date and known circumstances.
- AI sublimit inside or additional to the underlying limit.
- Defence inside or outside limits, counsel selection and settlement authority.
- Related-claims aggregation and coordination with E&O, Technology Products and Cyber.
- Material-change reporting, contractual consequences and applicable notice requirements.
- Confirmed reinsurance response, exclusions and treatment of common-vendor events.

Separate financial reserves for defence and indemnity do not themselves create enforceable contractual caps.

Break-even frequency is 2.75%; the frequency consistent with the illustrated 10% target margin is 2.25%. The model excludes separate reinsurance premiums/recoveries, taxes, investment returns and capital charges. These can materially reduce allowable frequency or require a higher price. Fixed cost timing and policy earning patterns also matter: the table assumes 100 full policy-years, not 100 policies issued partway through a launch year.

## Common-vendor stress

For a purely illustrative 20 affected policies with CAD 250,000 average covered cost each, gross cost is CAD 5M. Even unrelated SMEs can share one dependency. Compare this scenario with a separately approved risk budget and actual treaty terms; do not treat this scenario as a probability estimate or CAD 5M as a recommended budget.

Distinguish deployed limits, expected loss, stressed loss and capital. The sum of nominal policy limits is a useful exposure register, not a full capital model.

## Stages and decisions

| Stage | Timing proposed by the team | Decision evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Prepare | Months 0–3 | Customer discovery, willingness-to-pay tests, coverage mapping, technical pricing, capital/reinsurance and claims approval |
| Restricted binding | Months 3–6 | Verified use and oversight, complete dependencies, approved terms and capacity |
| Monitor | Monthly and quarterly after binding | Control drift, notifications, defence cost, rate adequacy, vendor concentration and reserve development |
| First renewal review | 12 months after binding, earliest Month 15 | Continue, reprice future terms, restrict or stop new business; expand only with sufficient mature evidence |

No binding where mandatory controls, wording, capacity or data requirements fail. Pause the affected new-business segment when an approved concentration threshold, risk budget or cost tolerance is breached. Management must approve actual numerical thresholds from its risk budget before launch; none is invented as a case fact.

Renewal is a useful decision point, not proof of mature long-tail profitability. Stopping new business does not remove prior-period obligations. The timing and permissibility of midterm changes depend on contract and law.

## When the recommendation changes

Choose a defined capability-building phase if credible customer willingness to pay remains below technical price, coverage cannot be distinguished from existing promises, common-event stress exceeds supported capacity, or operational/legal gates fail. Reconsider a narrow standalone product when demonstrable customer needs and economics justify a separate structure. Under equivalent scope, both endorsements and standalone products can use limits and exclusions.
